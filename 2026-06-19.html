1 Big Thing

Office CMBS delinquency hits ~12.1% — a record, and the "extend and pretend" era is closing

Driving the news

CMBS office loan delinquency reached approximately 12.1% in early 2026, topping the prior record of 11.76% set in October 2025 and more than doubling the pre-pandemic baseline. The delinquency spike is not primarily missed monthly payments — it is maturity defaults: sponsors with performing, cash-flowing assets who simply cannot refinance at today's rates against today's values.

By the numbers

  • $76.6B in CMBS loans face hard maturities in 2026 — office and retail carry the largest refinancing exposure
  • >50% of 2026 CMBS maturities expected to miss on-time payoff (CRE Daily / Trepp)
  • April conduit payoff rate: 67.46% — recovering from a 45.6% trough in Feb, but 1-in-3 loans still failing to pay off
  • Office appraisal values down 55.8% since issuance on distressed loans; DPOs running at 30–50% haircuts

Between the lines

Lenders are increasingly conditioning extensions on meaningful concessions — cash-in equity, hard reserves, principal paydowns. The post-pandemic era of costless maturity kicks is over. Loan modification, sponsor cash-in, and discounted payoffs (DPOs) are now the primary resolution tracks for office CMBS.

For dummies: Office buildings financed through CMBS bonds are defaulting at record rates — not because rents stopped coming in, but because loans are maturing and borrowers can't get new financing at today's rates and lower property values. For CWCAM, this means the special servicing queue is deeper, workout negotiations are harder, and DPO bids will keep coming at steep discounts.

Sources: Trepp, Wolf Street, KBRA, Commercial Real Estate Direct, CRE Daily

Retail Distress

Destiny USA's $480M CMBS note hits the market

What's happening

The CMBS loan against Destiny USA — New York State's largest mall, in Syracuse — was offered for note sale as of June 8. The loan has grown to approximately $480M, the lion's share of the property's $700M+ total debt stack, which also includes $250M in Syracuse IDA PILOT bonds.

Yes, but

This is a note sale, not an REO disposition — the special servicer is gauging investor appetite for a discounted loan purchase rather than pursuing direct foreclosure. Bid levels will reveal the market's current clearing price on large-format retail in secondary markets.

For dummies: The servicer is trying to sell the bad loan on Destiny USA at a discount rather than take the mall through foreclosure itself — whoever buys the note inherits the workout. Watch the bid-ask: it sets a comp for what struggling malls are worth right now.

Sources: Commercial Real Estate Direct (crenews.com, June 8, 2026); CNY Central

Office / Geography

Seattle office overtakes SF as worst vacancy nationally — CMBS exposure growing

Why it matters

Downtown Seattle office has surpassed San Francisco as the highest-vacancy office market in the U.S. — a market where $13.72B across 345 office loans are due to mature by year-end. Seattle's structural demand collapse (tech layoffs + remote persistence) makes refinancing nearly impossible for most in-market CMBS collateral.

Between the lines

Five- and ten-year loan cohorts from 2016 and 2021 are hitting maturities simultaneously in gateway office markets hardest hit by post-COVID demand shifts. Seattle, SF, and Chicago office CMBS carry disproportionate default risk through H2 2026.

For dummies: Seattle's office market is now worse than San Francisco's, which is saying something — and the loans coming due there mostly can't refinance. Special servicers should expect a wave of transfers from Pacific Northwest office collateral over the next 6–9 months.

Sources: Commercial Real Estate Direct (crenews.com, June 5, 2026); CRE Daily; Commercial Observer

Capital Markets

Special servicing rate dips to 10.86% — but don't pop the champagne

Driving the news

Trepp's May 2026 special servicing report showed the U.S. CMBS special servicing rate fell 51 bps month-over-month to 10.86%. The retreat was driven by several large office loans curing and a denominator effect — a larger total CMBS balance diluting the rate.

Yes, but

$2.9B in new loans — mostly office and retail — transferred to special servicing in the same month. The cures driving the headline improvement were one-off large-loan events, not a structural reversal. With office delinquency at record highs and the maturity wall still ahead, the rate is more likely to re-accelerate than sustain this dip.

For dummies: The special servicing rate ticked down last month, but mainly because some big loans cured — not because the market got healthier. The pipeline of new problem loans coming in is still large, and more maturities are coming all summer.

Sources: Trepp Special Servicing Report (May 2026); CRE Daily
