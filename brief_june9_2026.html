1 big thing

Office CMBS special servicing hits all-time record 16.38%

Driving the news: The office CMBS special servicing rate surged +62 bps in June to 16.38% — an all-time high, blowing past the peak set during the GFC. The overall CMBS special servicing rate hit 10.57%, the highest level since May 2013. Office delinquencies rose +49 bps to 11.08%, also surpassing the 2024 and 2012 peaks.

Why it matters: Office loans transferred to special servicers now represent the largest single driver of pipeline growth for CMBS workout teams. The combination of record SS rates and a still-elevated 10-year (4.42%) means loan modifications and term extensions — not payoffs — will dominate resolution activity through year-end.

  • Overall CMBS SS rate: 10.57% (highest since May 2013)
  • Office SS rate: 16.38% (all-time record, +62 bps MoM)
  • Office delinquency: 11.08% (+49 bps MoM; exceeds 2012 GFC peak)
  • Overall delinquency (S&P): 6.1% (May 2026)
For dummies:

More office loans are in trouble than at any point in history — including the financial crisis — which means special servicers are receiving more files, more often, with more complexity; expect your workout caseload to grow, not shrink, this year.

Sources: S&P Global Ratings SF Credit Brief (May 2026 delinquency); CMBS servicer data (June 2026 SS rates)
Workout watch

$647.5M 20 Times Square back in special servicing

Driving the news: Maefield Development's 20 Times Square CMBS loan (Times Square Trust 2018-20TS, originated by Natixis) missed its May 2026 maturity and has transferred back to special servicing for the second time since 2022.

Between the lines: The property sits atop a 99-year ground lease running through 2117, which significantly complicates any foreclosure or note sale — a receiver or buyer inherits a long-term lease structure, not fee simple. Four floors of retail remain largely vacant; income flows almost entirely from the 454-room Edition by Marriott hotel.

  • Debt: $647.5M (single-asset single-borrower, Times Square Trust 2018-20TS)
  • Prior SS: November 2022 – late 2023; received maturity extension
  • Collateral: 99-year ground lease + Edition hotel (454 rooms) + 4 floors vacant retail
  • Complication: Ground-lease structure limits clean workout/REO exit paths
For dummies:

A ground-lease CMBS deal with vacant retail and a repeat maturity default has no easy exit — the special servicer will likely pursue an extension or note sale rather than foreclosure, since taking title under a 99-year ground lease doesn't give you the building.

Sources: Commercial Observer; The Real Deal; Hoodline (May 2026)
Maturity wall

$300M Lakewood Center: largest June hard maturity in CMBS

Driving the news: The $300M loan on Lakewood Center mall (suburban LA) is Trepp's largest June hard maturity. Pacific Retail Capital Partners, which acquired the mall from Macerich, is in active workout negotiations with the lender — but no refi has been secured.

Yes, but: This is a capital-structure problem, not an operational one. The property carries a 1.33x DSCR and 93% occupancy — the asset is performing. The challenge is sourcing new financing at today's rates.

For dummies:

A cash-flowing, nearly full mall can't get a new loan because rates are too high — if workout talks stall, this becomes a special servicing transfer on a property that isn't actually broken, which is exactly the kind of maturity-default workout that defines the 2026 distress cycle.

Sources: The Real Deal LA (June 4, 2026); Trepp; CoStar
Macro

10-year at 4.42% — refi pain persists

By the numbers:

  • SOFR: 3.63% (June 5, 2026)
  • 10Y Treasury: 4.42%  |  30Y Treasury: 4.93%
  • Fed funds target: 3.50–3.75% (unchanged since December 2025)
  • Morgan Stanley estimates $500B of 2026 CRE maturities "at risk of refi failure"

Between the lines: With the Fed on hold and the 10-year above 4.40%, borrowers on the 2026 maturity wall face debt service coverage shortfalls even on modestly performing assets. YTD CMBS issuance is up 17% to ~$72B, but conduit AA/A spreads are widening (+10 bps each), signaling some investor fatigue at current volumes.

For dummies:

High rates are the reason healthy properties can't refinance and are ending up in special servicing — until the Fed cuts meaningfully, the maturity-default pipeline stays full regardless of property performance.

Sources: Federal Reserve H.15 (June 5); SOFR Academy; Morgan Stanley CRE Research
What I'm watching:

Whether Pacific Retail Capital Partners secures financing for Lakewood Center before the June maturity hard date — if a 93%-occupied, DSCR-positive mall can't refi in the current market, it confirms that the 2026 distress cycle is rate-driven and structural, not property-performance-driven, with major implications for mod/extension strategy across the portfolio.
